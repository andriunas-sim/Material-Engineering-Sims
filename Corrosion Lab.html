Model notes and assumptions

These are teaching models: each one is built to show the mechanism and the right order of magnitude. Treat any rate here as ±50 % at best.

Uniform

  • Steel plate: constant rate, default 0.1 mm/yr as on the slide. Life = corrosion allowance ÷ rate. The equivalent current density comes from Faraday's law with the slide's values: M = 56 g/mol, ρ = 7900 kg/m³, z = 2, F = 96,500 C/mol.
  • Rebar: the slide 17 calculation with the same constants, worked without intermediate rounding (5.93 years, 2,166 days; the slide's 2,162 days comes from rounding the moles to 4.84). Real rebar corrosion is often localised (chloride pitting), which is worse than this.
  • Rust is drawn at 3× the volume of the steel it came from (real rust is 2–6×). That expansion is what cracks the concrete cover.

Galvanic

  • Potentials are typical galvanic-series values in flowing seawater (V vs saturated calomel electrode). The cell is a circuit obeying Ohm's law: I = ΔE / (R_electrolyte + R_anode + R_cathode), with R_electrolyte = 20 Ω·cm × 0.5 cm⁻¹, R_anode = 1 kΩ·cm² / A_anode and R_cathode = 10 kΩ·cm² / A_cathode for oxygen reduction.
  • Rate from Faraday's law, CR [mm/yr] = 3.27×10⁻³ · i [µA/cm²] · M / (n·ρ), plus the anode's own corrosion. The cathode's own corrosion is suppressed by exp(−i_c / 3 µA·cm⁻²). Each electrode is a 2 mm plate; when 1 mm has gone from each face it is used up and the cell stops.

Pitting

  • PREN = %Cr + 3.3 %Mo + 16 %N with typical compositions: 304 (18Cr, 0.05N), 316 (17Cr, 2.1Mo, 0.05N), 2507 super duplex (25Cr, 3.8Mo, 0.27N).
  • The PREN needed is an indicative rule of thumb, 10 + 4·log₁₀[Cl⁻ mg/L] + 0.4·(T − 20 °C), chosen to agree with practice: 304 is fine in cool tap water, 316 pits in seawater, and PREN ≥ 40 is the usual requirement for seawater service.
  • Pit depth grows as 0.6 · (PREN needed − PREN) · √t mm with t in years; the 3 mm pipe wall leaks when the deepest pit reaches it. Pits are drawn and sized as undercut ellipsoids (width 0.6 × depth) to get the average loss, spread over a 9 × 9 mm patch of surface.

Crevice

  • Oxygen inside the crevice decays with a time constant of 0.2 days × (gap / 10 µm); wide gaps (beyond about 60 µm) keep exchanging water with the outside. The crevice then acidifies about 15× more slowly towards a pH that depends on the gap and temperature.
  • The passive film fails below a critical pH ≈ 4.0 − 0.1 × PREN (about 2.1 for 304 and 1.5 for 316, close to published crevice-solution values). After that the attack depth grows as 4 · (pH_crit − pH) · √t mm.

Flow

  • Below a critical speed the protective film survives and the rate rises only slightly as more oxygen arrives. The slide labels this region "laminar"; in real pipes flow is turbulent at almost any useful speed, and the boundary that matters is the speed at which wall shear strips the film. Critical speeds used: copper 1.5, nickel-aluminium bronze 4.5, 316 stainless 20 m/s. Sand and bubbles cut them by 40 %; a smoother shape raises them by 30 %.
  • Cavitation starts when the cavitation number σ = (p − p_v) / (½ρv²) falls below an inception value: 1.0 for a blunt shape, 0.7 for a smooth one, with p = 111 kPa (1 m under water) and p_v = 2.3 kPa. Damage then rises with v⁶, scaled by each alloy's resistance (copper 1, bronze 0.35, stainless 0.15).
